About this project

What it is

An asset allocation study built from public data. I rebuilt monthly total returns for US stocks (S&P 500 with dividends), 10-year Treasuries (from yields) and gold from January 1973 to September 2026, adjusted everything for inflation, and compared every mix of the three in 5% steps.

Why I built it

Almost every investing conversation starts with asset allocation, and the 60/40 portfolio comes up constantly. Instead of taking it on faith, I wanted to test it on more than 50 years of real returns, including the 1970s inflation, the 2008 crisis and 2022, when stocks and bonds fell together.

What I learned

  • Diversification works because the assets didn't move together: the stock–bond correlation was 0.05 over the period.
  • The asset that protects you changes: bonds in 2008, gold in the 1970s, and neither in 2022.
  • Data choices matter. Monthly-average prices hide how deep crashes really were, so I state that limit up front.

Method

  • Stocks: S&P 500 price changes plus dividends, from Robert Shiller's monthly dataset.
  • Bonds: the total return of a 10-year Treasury bought at par each month and repriced a month later at the new yield. This captures both the coupon and the price change.
  • Gold: monthly prices, starting in 1973 after the dollar's link to gold ended.
  • Real returns are deflated by US CPI. Volatility is annualized from monthly returns.

Limitations

  • Shiller's stock prices are monthly averages, not month-end prices. That smooths the numbers: the Covid drop shows as −16% here, while the real peak-to-trough fall was over 30%. Volatility and drawdowns for stocks are understated.
  • Dividends after June 2023 are estimated by holding the last reported dividend yield.
  • No fees, taxes or trading costs. Monthly rebalancing is assumed.
  • The past is one path. The 1973–2026 period included falling interest rates from 1981 to 2020, which flattered bonds.

Data: github.com/datasets (s-and-p-500, bond-yields-us-10y, gold-prices, cpi-us), sourced from Shiller, the Federal Reserve and the BLS.
