NLP for marketsPython · scikit-learn4,838 expert-labeled sentences

Off-the-shelf sentiment dictionaries do worse than always guessing "neutral" on financial news. A trained model does much better.

I compared two popular word lists with a classifier I trained on the Financial PhraseBank, a set of news sentences labeled positive, negative or neutral by finance-trained annotators. The trained model runs live in your browser below.

Model comparison on 968 held-out sentences

Macro F1 averages performance across the three classes, so a model can't score well by always guessing the most common label. The dictionaries miss finance-specific meaning. "Increase" and "rose" carry no tone in these word lists, but in an earnings sentence they're usually good news.

Which words the model learned

Push toward positive

Push toward negative

"Staff" and "personnel" lean negative because in this dataset they mostly appear in layoff news. A useful pattern, but also a reminder that the model learns correlations, not meaning.

Where it gets confused

About two-thirds of the mistakes (166 of 255) are mix-ups between neutral and positive. Only 17 of 121 negative sentences were called positive.

Method

  • Data: the 4,838 unique sentences where at least half the annotators agreed. Stratified 80/20 split with a fixed seed: 3,870 sentences for training, 968 for testing.
  • Dictionaries: VADER (general social-media lexicon) and Loughran–McDonald (built from 10-K filings), scored with a ±0.05 and ±0.1 polarity cutoff.
  • Trained model: TF-IDF on words and word pairs (4,000 features), with a class-balanced logistic regression.

Limitations

  • The sentences come from news about Finnish listed companies from the 2000s, so today's US headlines are slightly out of domain.
  • It counts words and word pairs, so phrasing it hasn't seen can fool it. It handles "loss narrowed," which appears in the training data, but a negation like "did not miss estimates" can trip it up.
  • This measures tone, not whether the news moves the price.
